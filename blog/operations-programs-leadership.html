Operations, Programs and Leadership Development: Where ToyBox Starts

1. Operations. How the work actually moves.

Operations is the part of an organization nobody sees until it breaks.

ToyBox Consulting & Management is an operations consulting firm in Bellevue, Washington. We work with nonprofits, public agencies and small businesses on three things: operations, programs, and leadership development. This post is about the first one.

Operations is how the work actually moves: who decides what, how a task gets from one person to the next, where the information lives, and which tools the team trusts. When it works, nobody talks about it. When it doesn't, everyone feels it.

Five signs the work has outgrown the systems behind it. The same question gets three different answers. Every decision waits on one person. New staff take months to feel useful because nothing is written down. Reports get rebuilt from scratch every time a funder or board asks. Busy seasons feel like emergencies instead of seasons.

None of that means the team is failing. It usually means the organization grew and the systems didn't grow with it. The fix starts smaller than most people think: map how work moves today, before buying new software or adding staff.

2. Programs. Defining what success looks like.

A program can be busy every single day and still not know if it's working.

Programs are the second of the three areas ToyBox works in, alongside operations and leadership development. The question underneath every program is the same: what is supposed to change, for whom, and how will you know?

That's what a theory of change answers. It's the "because" behind a program. If we do these activities, then these changes will happen for these people, because of this reason. Written down, it turns a good intention into something a team can plan around, a funder can understand, and a board can hold.

The most common gap ToyBox sees is a program that tracks what it did instead of what changed. One hundred workshops held is activity. Participants still in stable housing a year later is an outcome. Funders, boards and communities increasingly ask for the second one, and it's much easier to report when it's defined before the first participant walks in.

Try this: If your program had to prove one change a year from now, what would it be?

3. Leadership Development. Growing the next layer of leaders.

When every decision runs through the founder, growth waits in line.

Leadership development is the third area ToyBox works in, alongside operations and programs. One of the most common patterns in growing nonprofits and small businesses is the founder who becomes the bottleneck without meaning to. They built it, they know it best, and slowly every question finds its way back to them.

The cost shows up quietly. Staff wait for answers. Capable people stop making calls on their own. The founder can't take a real vacation, and the organization can't grow past what one person can hold.

Leadership development isn't only training. It's deciding which decisions belong at which level, writing down how the organization decides so it doesn't live in one person's head, and coaching the next layer of leaders to make those calls with confidence and back-up.

Here's a simple test. If the founder took two weeks off, which decisions would stall? That list is where leadership development starts.

4. How to build a logic model on one page.

A logic model fits a whole program on one page. Here's how to build one.

Start with five columns.

  • Inputs are what you have: staff, funding, space, partners, time.
  • Activities are what you do with them: the classes, the case management, the outreach.
  • Outputs are what you can count: sessions held, people served, meals delivered.
  • Short-term outcomes are what changes first: skills, knowledge, access.
  • Long-term outcomes are what changes for good: stable housing, steady income, safer streets.

Then check it backwards. Start at the long-term outcome and ask, does the column to the left actually lead here? If an activity doesn't connect to any outcome, it's either missing a link or it doesn't belong in this program.

The most common mistake ToyBox sees is stopping at outputs. Counting people served feels like proof, but funders and boards are asking what changed for those people. A logic model makes that gap visible before the report is due, not after.

A logic model is the one-page version of a theory of change, and it's the backbone of program design.

5. How do consultants get hired by a city? Often through a roster.

How do consultants actually get hired by a city? Often through a consultant roster.

A consultant roster, sometimes called a consultant pool, is a list of firms a government has already reviewed for certain kinds of work. When a department needs help, it can bring in a firm from the roster without running a brand-new procurement every time. For smaller projects, that saves months.

Getting on a roster usually starts with a Request for Qualifications, not a bid. The city wants to know who you are, what you've done and whether you're qualified, before price comes up at all.

ToyBox Consulting & Management was selected to 10 City of Seattle consultant rosters for December 2025 through December 2030, including Management Services, Project Management, Human Resources and Training and Counseling Services. All ten are small-business rosters.

If you run a small firm and you're curious about public sector work, watch your city's procurement page for roster solicitations. They tend to open on a schedule, and they reward clear, specific qualifications over polish.
